STRATIFICATION & INEQUALITY LAB · V4

How advantage accumulates

Explore stylized models of inequality, mobility, educational opportunity, and social networks. All generated populations and parameters are synthetic—not empirical estimates. Change assumptions and inspect the consequences.

01 · DISTRIBUTION

Wealth distribution simulator

A synthetic population of 1,000 households is generated from a lognormal wealth-like distribution. All values are nonnegative model units, not actual household net worth. Increasing dispersion raises concentration in this simplified model.

The Gini coefficient ranges from 0 (equal distribution) toward 1 (greater concentration). This simulator does not represent negative net worth or institutional causes of inequality.

02 · INTERGENERATIONAL MOBILITY

Class mobility model

A three-class Markov model illustrates how mobility probabilities affect the distribution of adult class positions across generations. Transition rates are hypothetical and held constant.

Openness is a modeling parameter, not a measured mobility index. A three-class model omits within-class inequality, population change, and structural shifts.

03 · CUMULATIVE ADVANTAGE

Compounding resources

Compare two hypothetical households with different starting resources and annual growth rates. Growth is deterministic and ignores taxes, shocks, consumption, and uncertainty.

04 · EDUCATIONAL OPPORTUNITY

Opportunity and attainment

An illustrative logistic model relates preparation, access to educational resources, and barriers to a simulated probability of completing a hypothetical credential. These are invented coefficients, not estimates of student outcomes.

05 · SOCIAL NETWORKS

Connections and opportunity

A small synthetic network illustrates bridging ties and access to information. Nodes are fictional people; edges represent undirected ties. Adding bridges can connect otherwise separated groups but does not guarantee equitable outcomes.

RESEARCH METHODS & STATISTICAL INFERENCE · V6

From samples to defensible conclusions

Explore sampling distributions, confidence intervals, hypothesis testing, regression, and experimental design. All demonstrations use synthetic data and idealized assumptions. Statistical significance is not equivalent to practical importance or causality.

01 · SAMPLING

Sampling distributions & uncertainty

Draw repeated independent random samples from a fixed synthetic population of 10,000 values. Compare sampling variability at different sample sizes.

Sampling here is with replacement, from a fixed population. This demonstration does not model survey nonresponse, clustering, or complex survey weights.

02 · CONFIDENCE INTERVALS

Repeated-interval coverage

For each random sample, calculate a two-sided interval for the mean using a normal critical value and the sample standard deviation. The nominal 95% level describes the long-run behavior of this procedure under its assumptions.

Normal critical values are used as an approximation, not Student's t. Intervals use independent simple random samples; results are not appropriate for weighted or clustered designs without modification.

03 · HYPOTHESIS TESTING

One-sample test for a mean

Specify a hypothetical observed sample mean, reference mean, sample standard deviation, and sample size. The app calculates an approximate two-sided z statistic and p-value using a normal approximation.

04 · REGRESSION

Ordinary least squares (OLS)

Use the two numeric variables currently loaded in Data Studio, or generate a synthetic dataset. This simple linear regression estimates an association, not a causal effect.

05 · EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN

Random assignment & confounding

Simulate 400 fictional participants. Randomized assignment makes treatment independent of baseline covariates in expectation; observational assignment can create confounding. Results vary by random draw.

SOCIAL DEMOGRAPHY · V7

Population, generations, and social change

Explore age structure, fertility, mortality, migration, demographic transitions, and population projections. All model populations and rates below are synthetic; they are not Census estimates or forecasts.

01 · AGE STRUCTURE

Interactive population pyramid

The age profiles are illustrative. The sex-ratio parameter changes the relative distribution of synthetic male and female births, not observed demographic patterns.

02 · COMPONENTS OF CHANGE

Births, deaths, and net migration

Annual population change = births − deaths + net migration. Crude birth and death rates are per 1,000 people per year.

This is a constant-rate, single-population arithmetic model with annual compounding. It does not track cohorts, age-specific fertility, life tables, capacity constraints, or changes in rates.

03 · COHORT-COMPONENT MODEL

Age-specific survival, fertility, and migration

A simplified five-year cohort-component projection moves people through five-year age bands, applies approximate survival and births, and distributes migration. Rates are hypothetical and remain constant.

This model illustrates cohort accounting, not a validated demographic forecast. The oldest age band is open-ended, and rates are stylized.

04 · DEMOGRAPHIC TRANSITION

Interpret population change historically

The demographic transition model is a heuristic: real trajectories differ across societies and are shaped by public health, gender relations, migration, policy, economic change, and historical circumstances.
